Brazil’s National Institute for Space Research, known as INPE, has measured how much of the Amazon is cleared each year since 1988 with a satellite survey called PRODES. The worst year on record was 1995, when about 11,200 square miles were cleared, and 2004 came close behind at 10,700, an area about the size of Massachusetts.

Brazil then expanded satellite monitoring, created new protected areas and stepped up fines for illegal clearing. By 2012 the yearly loss had fallen to 1,765 square miles, 84 percent below the 2004 level and still the lowest year in the survey.

The gains did not hold. Clearing climbed for most of the next decade and reached 5,034 square miles in 2021, nearly three times the 2012 low. Since then it has fallen four years in a row, to 2,238 square miles in 2025, down 56 percent from 2021 and the third-lowest year since 1988.

INPE also runs a faster system called DETER, which flags new clearing every day so enforcement teams can respond. Its alerts for August 2025 through July 2026 covered 1,110 square miles, more than a third below the year before and the lowest total since the current system started in 2015. That is a bit smaller than Rhode Island.

Five of the six states with the most alerts saw declines. Pará still led with 381 square miles, followed by Mato Grosso. Roraima moved the other way, with alerts up 32.5 percent, a reminder that the improvement is uneven.

Why the alerts matter

DETER is built for speed rather than precision, so its totals run lower than the official PRODES rate, which INPE calculates from sharper images after the dry season ends. The two systems usually move in the same direction, though. In 2025, DETER alerts fell and the PRODES rate followed with an 11 percent drop. If that pattern holds, the official figure for 2026 has a real chance of beating the 2012 record low of 1,765 square miles.

Brazil’s government and outside researchers point mainly to enforcement. Brazil’s Amazon action plan, first launched in 2004, lost its funding in 2019 and was restarted in 2023 along with higher penalties for environmental crimes. Clearing also fell this year in the Cerrado, the savanna south of the Amazon, where alerts dropped about 8 percent.

What’s next

INPE usually publishes its first PRODES estimate for the year in November. A strong El Niño is expected to bring drought to the region into early 2027, which raises the risk of fire, and fire damage is counted separately from clearing. Brazil also holds a presidential runoff on Oct. 25, and the two candidates disagree about how far to go in regulating land use.
