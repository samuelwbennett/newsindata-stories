A classic Thanksgiving dinner for 10 costs $58.63 this year, up 6% from $55.18 last year, according to the American Farm Bureau Federation’s 41st annual survey. That works out to $5.86 a person. Most of the increase is the bird: a 16-pound frozen turkey costs $23.68, up $2.18, or 10%.

Eight of the 12 items cost more, led by the turkey, whole milk (up 32 cents) and dinner rolls (up 25 cents). Pie crusts, frozen peas, whipping cream and the veggie tray got slightly cheaper. The dinner costs most in the Northeast ($61.57) and least in the South ($54.33). Adding a boneless ham, russet potatoes and frozen green beans brings the total to $81.72.

What the dinner costs in work

Prices are only half the story. Average hourly pay for production and nonsupervisory workers, who make up most of the private-sector workforce, rose about 3% over the past year, to $32.75 in October. At that rate the dinner takes about 1 hour and 47 minutes of work. That is 3 minutes more than last year, but less than in any other year since the survey began in 1986, when it took 3 hours and 11 minutes.

The price on the receipt peaked at $64.05 in 2022, when food inflation was at its highest in decades. This year’s total is 8% below that record. Adjusted for inflation, the 1986 dinner would cost $87.85 today.

How we calculated: Prices are from the American Farm Bureau Federation’s 2026 survey, in which volunteer shoppers in all 50 states and Puerto Rico recorded grocery prices, and its 2025 survey; item prices for 2026 are as reported by The Boston Report from the survey. Totals for earlier years are from the Farm Bureau’s survey history. Minutes of work divide each year’s total by average hourly earnings of production and nonsupervisory employees in November (October for 2026). Inflation adjustments use the consumer price index for all urban consumers, in October 2026 dollars. Both series are from the Bureau of Labor Statistics via FRED.
