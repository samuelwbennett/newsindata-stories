Two teams get a home game every Thanksgiving. Detroit has hosted since 1934 and Dallas since 1966, and between them they have played 144 holiday games. On Thursday the Lions host the Bears and the Cowboys host the Eagles. Their records on the day could hardly be more different: Dallas is 35-22-1, and Detroit is 38-46-2.

It was not always this lopsided. Detroit’s running record reached five games over .500 in 2000. Since 2001 the Lions are 6-19 on Thanksgiving, including nine straight losses from 2004 to 2012 and a 47-10 loss to Tennessee in 2008, the year they went 0-16. The Cowboys are 14-11 over the same stretch. They first reached 13 games over .500 in 2009 and got back there last year, when they beat Kansas City 31-28 in the most-watched regular-season game in NFL history.

Every game, side by side

Detroit’s best decade was the 1950s, when it went 8-2 and played Green Bay every Thanksgiving from 1951 to 1963. In 1962 the Lions beat the Packers 26-14, Green Bay’s only loss on the way to a 13-1 season. Dallas has its own lore: in 1974 a backup quarterback, Clint Longley, came off the bench to beat Washington, and in 1993 Leon Lett touched a blocked field goal in the snow, giving Miami the ball and the win.

Why the hosts keep their games

The Lions’ 0-16 season in 2008 brought calls to replace them with a more attractive matchup. The league kept them, and the calls faded as Detroit’s teams improved in the 2020s. Dallas, usually the bigger television draw, has faced far fewer such calls. Some critics argue that hosting is itself an advantage, since the home team skips a travel day on a short week.

No other team comes close in volume. Among franchises with at least 10 Thanksgiving games, only the Cowboys, Bears (20-16-2) and Bills (6-4-1) have winning records, and the Cardinals, Washington and Broncos have worse records than Detroit.
