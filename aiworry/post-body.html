When Pew Research Center first asked Americans in November 2021 how the increased use of artificial intelligence in daily life made them feel, 37% said they were more concerned than excited and 18% said they were more excited than concerned, about two to one. In Pew’s newest survey, conducted June 22-28, 2026, 52% said they were more concerned and 9% said they were more excited, a ratio of more than five to one. Another 37% said they felt both about equally.

Most of the shift came in one jump. The share more concerned than excited went from 38% in Pew’s 2022 survey to 52% in 2023, the first reading after ChatGPT’s public release in November 2022. It has stayed at 50% or higher in every survey since, while the share more excited has fallen by half, from 18% to 9%.

Both parties

The gap shows up on both sides of the aisle. In the 2026 survey, 56% of Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents and 49% of Republicans and Republican leaners said they were more concerned than excited. About one in 10 in each party said they were more excited.

Young adults turned the most

In 2021, adults under 30 were the least concerned age group: 31% were more concerned than excited and 25% were more excited than concerned. By 2026, 55% of them were more concerned and 11% were more excited. It was the first time a majority of under-30s told Pew they were mainly concerned. Adults 65 and older are still the most concerned group, at 59%.

The answer depends little on the question

Pew’s question offers a middle option, and more than a third of Americans take it. Max Roser of Our World in Data compared six national surveys that ask about AI in different ways and found that the negative side wins in every one.

In a Quinnipiac poll in March, 55% expected AI to do more harm than good in their day-to-day lives and 34% expected more good. In an Ipsos poll for ABC News and The Washington Post in October, Americans split almost evenly on whether AI is a good or bad thing for them personally, 50% to 49%, but at the extremes 15% called it extremely bad and 3% extremely good. For society, 17% said extremely bad and 2% extremely good. In a Gallup survey with Bentley University in May, 9% chose the positive option, the same share as in Pew’s.

How the U.S. compares

Americans are more worried than most of the world. In Pew’s 2026 global survey of 37 countries, a median of 37% said they were more concerned than excited, 15 points below the U.S. figure. Concern tended to run higher in wealthier countries; Australia, Canada, Greece, Italy and Poland stood out along with the U.S., and Israel was the only country where more people were mainly excited than mainly concerned.

Pew did not survey China. Ipsos did ask one question in 30 countries in spring 2025, plus China, whose results Ipsos provided to Stanford’s AI Index: do products and services using AI have more benefits than drawbacks? In China, 87% agreed. In the U.S., 42% did, below the 30-country average of 56% and below Italy and Spain (53%), Germany (49%) and Britain (43%). France and Belgium, at 41%, were slightly lower.

Two cautions apply to the China figure. Ipsos surveys online, and in countries where internet access is less universal its online samples tend to be more urban, educated and affluent than the population as a whole. And a single agree-or-disagree question can’t tell enthusiasm from resignation, a point Zilan Qian made in a May 2026 essay for ChinaTalk on what Chinese AI optimism does and doesn’t show.

Ipsos’s numbers also show positive views rising in much of Europe. Since 2022, the share seeing more benefits than drawbacks rose 12 points in Germany and 10 in France, according to the AI Index. In the U.S., it rose from 35% to 42%, even as Pew’s measure of concern climbed. The two polls ask different things: one about products and services, the other about AI’s growing role in daily life.
