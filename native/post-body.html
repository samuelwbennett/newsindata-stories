When the Wampanoag joined the English colonists at Plymouth for the harvest feast of 1621 that Americans now remember as the first Thanksgiving, the Native peoples of North America numbered somewhere between about 1 million and 18 million. Scholars still disagree about which end of that range is right. They agree on what came next: disease, war, forced removal and enslavement shrank that population for nearly three centuries.

By 1900 the census counted 266,732 American Indians and Alaska Natives in the United States, the lowest in any census since the first nationwide count of Native people in 1890. The demographer Russell Thornton, whose 1987 estimate put the population north of Mexico at about 7 million before contact, including about 5 million in what is now the United States, estimated the low point for the U.S., Canada and Greenland together at about 375,000.

The recovery

The count began to climb in the 20th century and took off after 1960, when the census began relying on people to report their own race rather than leaving it to a census taker. It reached 1.4 million in 1980 and nearly 2 million in 1990. Some of that growth came from births and longer lives, and some from more people identifying as Native, Thornton wrote.

In 2000 the census first let people choose more than one race, which created two ways to count. In 2020, 3.7 million people named only American Indian or Alaska Native, and 9.7 million named it alone or with another race, 2.9% of the country. The federal government recognizes more than 570 tribal nations.

What we got wrong in 2016

We first charted this in November 2016. That version used a single pre-contact estimate of 5 million, called a 2015 Census Bureau estimate a census and compared Native Americans with the white population on one chart, which flattened the Native line. This version shows the full range of scholarly estimates and the census counts both ways.
