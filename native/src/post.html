When the Wampanoag joined the English colonists at Plymouth for the harvest feast of 1621 that Americans now remember as the first Thanksgiving, the Native peoples of North America numbered somewhere between about 1 million and 18 million. Scholars still disagree about which end of that range is right. They agree on what came next: disease, war, forced removal and enslavement shrank that population for nearly three centuries.

By 1900 the census counted 266,732 American Indians and Alaska Natives in the United States, the lowest in any census since the first nationwide count of Native people in 1890. The demographer Russell Thornton, whose 1987 estimate put the population north of Mexico at about 7 million before contact, including about 5 million in what is now the United States, estimated the low point for the U.S., Canada and Greenland together at about 375,000.

A mark in Antarctic ice

The losses were so large that they may have changed the climate. In a 2019 study, researchers at University College London estimated that about 60 million people lived in North and South America before 1492 and that roughly 90% of them died within a century. As farms and villages were abandoned, about 56 million hectares, an area roughly the size of France, grew back into forest and drew carbon out of the air.

Air bubbles trapped in the ice at Law Dome, Antarctica, show carbon dioxide falling about 11 parts per million from the early 1500s to the 1610s, the lowest level in the record’s 2,000 years. The study attributes about half of that decline to regrowth in the Americas and other changes in land use, and estimates that it cooled the planet by about 0.15 degrees Celsius during one of the coldest stretches of the Little Ice Age. Some scientists have proposed the dip, which they call the Orbis spike, as the start of the Anthropocene, the age of human influence on the Earth.

The recovery

The count began to climb in the 20th century and took off after 1960, when the census began relying on people to report their own race rather than leaving it to a census taker. It reached 1.4 million in 1980 and nearly 2 million in 1990. Some of that growth came from births and longer lives, and some from more people identifying as Native, Thornton wrote.

In 2000 the census first let people choose more than one race, which created two ways to count. In 2020, 3.7 million people named only American Indian or Alaska Native, and 9.7 million named it alone or with another race, 2.9% of the country. The federal government recognizes more than 570 tribal nations.

The two counts also differ sharply from tribe to tribe. Most people who named the Navajo Nation in the 2020 census named nothing else. Most who named Cherokee also named another race or tribe.

Census answers and tribal citizenship measure different things. Tribes set their own membership rules, often based on descent from people listed on historical rolls, while the census counts anyone who writes in a tribe. The Navajo Nation reported 399,494 enrolled citizens in 2021, close to the 423,412 people who named it in the census. The Cherokee Nation reported about 450,000 citizens in 2023, while about 1.5 million people named Cherokee.
