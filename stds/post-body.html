In November 2015, this site covered a CDC report that put chlamydia at an all-time high, more than 1.44 million cases in 2014, with gonorrhea and syphilis near 10-year highs. The climb didn't stop there. Chlamydia peaked in 2019, gonorrhea in 2021 and the newest syphilis infections in 2022.

Since then, most of those numbers have come down. In 2024, the combined count of chlamydia, gonorrhea and syphilis fell 9%, to about 2.25 million cases, the third straight yearly decline, according to provisional CDC data. One number kept going up: 3,941 babies were born with syphilis, the 12th straight annual increase and eight times the 2015 count.

Where the declines are

The chlamydia rate fell to 445.7 cases per 100,000 people in 2024, 19% below its 2019 peak and slightly below where it was in 2015. Gonorrhea fell to 159.8 per 100,000, 25% below its 2021 peak but still 30% above 2015.

The steepest drop was in primary and secondary syphilis, the earliest and most contagious stages, which fell 22% in a single year. Most of that drop came among men who have sex with men, whose cases fell from 20,004 in 2022 to 12,855 in 2024. The CDC credits several things: doxy-PEP, a dose of the antibiotic doxycycline taken within 72 hours after sex, which it began recommending for some gay and bisexual men and transgender women in 2024; new at-home tests; and faster point-of-care testing.

Where they aren't

Syphilis among women is following a different path. Men's cases fell in every stage of the disease from 2023 to 2024. Women's primary and secondary cases fell too, but cases of early syphilis past those first stages rose 7.9%, and late or unknown-duration cases rose 4.1%. That matters for newborns, because a pregnant woman with untreated syphilis at any stage can pass it to her baby.

Congenital syphilis can cause miscarriage, stillbirth and infant death, and babies who survive can be left with blindness, deafness or bone deformities. It's almost entirely preventable: a blood test during pregnancy and penicillin before delivery. A 2023 CDC analysis found that most cases involved a missed or late test or inadequate treatment during pregnancy.

The increase has slowed. Newborn cases rose 31% in 2022, then 3% in 2023 and 2% in 2024. But 3,941 cases is still about 110 for every 100,000 babies born, up from about 12 in 2015.

Is the decline real?

Probably, but with less certainty than usual. Surveillance counts only positive tests, so fewer cases can also mean fewer people were tested. Many health departments have cut sexual health clinic hours and staff in recent years. And the 2024 figures are provisional: the CDC released only national totals, holding back state, age and race breakdowns while states move to a new reporting system. The agency expects final 2024 data, with those details, later in 2026, and says it doesn't expect the national trends to change much.

Even with the declines, the combined total of 2.25 million cases is 13% higher than a decade earlier, and newborn syphilis is nearly 700% higher, by the CDC's count.
