Seventy-two percent of Americans say it is extremely or very important for parents to vaccinate their children, according to a Gallup poll released Nov. 24. That is up slightly from 69% in 2024, the first increase since Gallup began asking in 2001, but far below the 94% who said so then.

The dip in support has come alongside a slide in the shots themselves. In the 2025-26 school year, 92.4% of kindergartners had both doses of the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine, the lowest in CDC records going back to 2009-10 and below the 95% that public health officials say keeps measles from spreading. The CDC has confirmed 5,838 measles cases this year as of Nov. 19, the most since 1991. On Nov. 17, the U.S. lost the measles elimination status it had held since 2000.

A gap that opened after 2019

The share calling childhood vaccines extremely important rose to 47%, from 40% in 2024. Two-thirds of Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents say so, compared with 27% of Republicans and Republican-leaning independents. That 39-point gap is close to the 37 points in 2024 and far wider than the four to 15 points Gallup measured in 2001, 2015 and 2019. Democrats’ views have barely moved; the share of Republicans calling vaccines extremely important fell from 52% in 2019 to 26% in 2024.

The same split shows up on safety. Seventeen percent of Americans think vaccines are more dangerous than the diseases they prevent, down from 20% in 2024 but up from 6% to 11% in Gallup’s earlier polls. Among Republicans, 32% say so, up from 12% in 2019, compared with 5% of Democrats. A majority of Americans, 56%, say the government should require parents to vaccinate their children against diseases like measles, up from 51% in 2024 but well below the 81% who said so in a 1991 poll.

The autism question

Sixteen percent of Americans say vaccines cause autism, the highest in Gallup’s four readings since 2015, while 46% say they do not and 38% are unsure. Among Republicans, 29% say vaccines cause autism, up from 19% in 2024; 53% are unsure. Large studies have found no link, including a 2019 Danish study of more than 650,000 children. In November 2025, Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. directed the CDC to change its website to say a link had not been ruled out.
