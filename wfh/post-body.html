About 21.3 million American workers mostly worked from home in 2025, according to new Census Bureau estimates. That's 12.9% of all workers, the lowest share since the pandemic and the fourth straight yearly decline. It's also still more than twice the 2019 share, when about 9 million people, or 5.7%, worked from home.

Start with a guess.

The decline has been steep at first and slow since. The share fell 2.7 points from 2021 to 2022, then 1.4 points, then 0.5, then 0.4 in 2025. At that pace, remote work is settling well above where it started, not drifting back to 2019.

Washington, D.C., went back to the office the most

In 2021, nearly half of workers in Washington, D.C., 48.3%, worked from home. By 2025 it was 22.7%, a drop of 25.6 points, more than twice as large as any state's. D.C. still has the highest share in the country, but the gap has narrowed.

The states that fell furthest after D.C. were mostly along the East Coast: Maryland dropped 10.3 points, Massachusetts 9.5 and New Jersey 9.1. Each has a large office workforce that commuted into big cities before 2020. Florida fell only 1.1 points, from 16.6% to 15.5%, and South Carolina was nearly unchanged. Only Wyoming and Mississippi had higher shares in 2025 than in 2021.

Colorado leads the states

Among the 50 states, Colorado has the highest share, 18.8%, followed by Arizona at 16.2% and Vermont at 15.9%. The lowest are North Dakota at 6.3%, Mississippi at 6.5% and Alaska at 7.1%, less than half Colorado's rate.

Boulder, Colo., has the highest share of any metro area the Census published, 24.9%, about one in four workers. Raleigh, N.C., is second at 22.8%, then Austin at 22.1%, Wilmington, N.C., at 21.2% and Denver at 21.1%. At the other end, fewer than one in 20 workers in Terre Haute, Ind., works from home.

Tech hubs are cooling, Florida isn't

Since metro boundaries were last redrawn in 2023, some of the biggest declines have come in places built on office and tech jobs. In the San Jose metro, the heart of Silicon Valley, the share fell from 16.2% to 12.6%, below the national rate. The Washington, D.C., metro fell from 21.9% to 17.4%, and Charlotte from 21.5% to 17.3%.

Several Florida metros moved the other way. Deltona-Daytona Beach rose from 14.7% to 17.4%, North Port-Sarasota from 16.0% to 18.2% and Lakeland from 13.7% to 15.6%. Metro estimates have margins of error of one to three points, so year-to-year moves smaller than that may be noise; the finder above shows each metro's margin.

How we counted: Figures are from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey 1-year estimates, table B08301 (Means of Transportation to Work), released Oct. 29, 2026. "Worked from home" counts workers 16 and older who worked from home most days in the week before they were surveyed; people who split their week between home and an office are usually counted by the way they commuted most of that week. The 2019 national figure is from the Census Bureau's Sept. 15, 2022, release; there is no standard 1-year estimate for 2020. State figures compare 2021 and 2025.
